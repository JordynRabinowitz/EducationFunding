01
Current Status of the Debate

What is being debated now—and why should teachers care?

California has moved the debate over paid student teaching from a proposal to an active implementation question. Beginning in the 2026–27 academic year, eligible candidates can receive a $10,000 Student Teacher Stipend. In July 2026, AB 126 increased program funding for 2026–27 from $100 million to $125 million. The policy now raises practical questions about access, implementation, long-term funding, and whether candidates in high-need credential areas should receive additional support. (California Commission on Teacher Credentialing [CTC], 2026b)

Assignment Requirement: Current status + impact on teaching
Central Policy Question

Should we pay to prepare the next generation of teachers?

California has already begun answering part of this question by funding student-teacher stipends. The larger debate is whether financial support for teacher preparation should become a stable part of the education system, how much support candidates should receive, and whether public investment in preparation can strengthen recruitment, equity, and retention.

California has moved from proposal to implementation

The Student Teacher Stipend Program is now active for eligible unpaid student teachers during clinical practice. That shifts the policy debate toward whether the program should become a stable part of teacher preparation, who should qualify, and whether the support is enough to reduce barriers to entering the profession. (CTC, 2026b)

The financial barrier is larger than tuition alone

California requires teacher-preparation programs to include at least 600 hours of clinical practice. Research on California teacher candidates estimates a typical annual cost of attendance of about $32,000 at CSU programs and $54,000 at UC programs, including tuition, housing, transportation, and other living costs. Extended clinical practice is often unpaid, making outside work difficult for many candidates. (CTC, 2026a) (Leung-Gagné et al., 2025)

Funding is connected to the teacher shortage

California still faces persistent shortages of fully prepared teachers. In 2024–25, close to 8,000 emergency-style permits were issued, and more than one-third of new teachers entered through an emergency-style permit. Shortages and underprepared staffing are concentrated in higher-need schools, making the pipeline a student-equity issue as well as a workforce issue. (Leung-Gagné et al., 2026)

The debate is shifting toward sustainability and targeting

The unresolved questions now include whether stipend funding will remain stable over time, whether every candidate should receive the same amount, and whether shortage fields should receive more. AB 126 provides for an additional $5,000 high-need-field stipend beginning in 2027–28, to the extent funds are available, for fields that may include mathematics, science, technology, bilingual/computer science authorizations, and special education. (CTC, 2026b)

Financial Barriers to Becoming a Teacher

The cost of preparation can determine who is able to enter teaching. California requires at least 600 hours of clinical practice, and because student teaching is often an unpaid commitment completed during the regular school day, maintaining another job can be extremely difficult. In a recent California survey, more than 40% of Golden State Teacher Grant recipients said they would not have completed preparation without the grant; candidates with greater financial need were even more likely to say this. These barriers make the cost of becoming a teacher a policy issue rather than only an individual expense. (CTC, 2026a) (Leung-Gagné et al., 2025)

Why This Matters for Schools & Students

Teacher retention is also tied to funding. California research finds that higher turnover is associated with lower district salaries, heavier workloads, greater reliance on underprepared teachers, and lower per-pupil spending. Students in California's highest-need schools are more likely to experience underprepared teachers and higher turnover, so funding the preparation and retention of fully credentialed teachers affects students' access to stable, qualified educators. (Leung-Gagné et al., 2026)

02
Brief History & Origins

How did California arrive at its current school-funding system?

California's current system grew out of repeated debates over local property wealth, statewide responsibility, equity, and local control. These turning points help explain why California now relies heavily on state funding and why teacher-pipeline investments can become statewide policy decisions.

Assignment Requirement: Brief history + timeline
1971

Serrano v. Priest

Los Angeles County public-school parents challenged California's heavy reliance on local property taxes because differences in property wealth created large funding disparities between districts. The California Supreme Court concluded that educational opportunity could not constitutionally depend on district wealth, pushing California toward greater state-driven equalization. (Serrano v. Priest, 1971)

1978

Proposition 13

Proposition 13 capped the basic property-tax rate at 1% and limited annual increases in assessed value. Local property-tax revenue fell sharply, reducing districts' independent tax-raising capacity and making California more dependent on the state General Fund for K–12 operating revenue. (Legislative Analyst's Office [LAO], 2012)

1988

Proposition 98

Proposition 98 is a voter-approved constitutional amendment that establishes a guaranteed minimum annual funding level for K–12 schools and community colleges. Its formulas tie the guarantee to factors including state General Fund revenue, personal income, and student attendance. (LAO, 2026b)

2013

Local Control Funding Formula

LCFF replaced the older revenue-limit system and many narrow categorical programs with a simpler model built around local flexibility and student need. It provides a base grant per student plus additional funding for English learners, low-income students, foster youth, and other unduplicated high-need students. (California Department of Education [CDE], n.d.)

Now

Teacher-Pipeline Investments

California has increasingly invested directly in educator preparation through programs such as the Golden State Teacher Grant, teacher residencies, and paid clinical-practice supports. Beginning July 1, 2026, the Student Teacher Stipend Program made a $10,000 stipend available to eligible unpaid student teachers through participating programs and LEAs. (Leung-Gagné et al., 2025) (CTC, 2026b)

03
California & Orange County

What makes this funding debate different here?

California is different from many places because the state has set aside public funding for student-teacher stipends, but the stipend is not automatically implemented in every district or charter school. Orange County operates within this statewide system, so a candidate's ability to receive the stipend can still depend on whether the educator-preparation program and placement LEA participate. (California Commission on Teacher Credentialing [CTC], n.d.)

Assignment Requirement: How CA / OC differs from other places
CA State Context

California

California has made paid student teaching a statewide policy issue. The state created a $10,000 stipend for eligible unpaid student teachers, but candidates cannot apply directly. Their preparation program and placement local education agency must participate in the state process before the stipend can reach them. (CTC, 2026b) (CTC, n.d.)

OC Local Context

Orange County

Orange County does not have one separate stipend policy; it operates within California's statewide program. Multiple Orange County LEAs appeared on an August 2026 application list, showing that the program is being used in the region. At the same time, access can still vary by placement because local participation is part of the implementation process. (Noonoo, 2026)

Why the Rollout Is Uneven

State funding does not guarantee local access.

The Student Teacher Stipend Program is funded by California, but local implementation matters. This means the state can set aside money for student teachers while candidates in different placements still experience different levels of access.

No Direct Student Applications

Student teachers cannot sign up for the grant themselves. Their educator-preparation program and the district or charter school where they are placed must participate in the application process, and the LEA receives and distributes the stipend funds. (CTC, n.d.)

High Administrative Burden

Districts must verify required information and determine how to pay student teachers who may not otherwise be district employees, without separate state funding to manage the paperwork. EdSource reported that some districts may decide the administrative burden is too high, which can leave otherwise eligible student teachers without access to the stipend. (EdSource, 2026)

A Brand-New System

Because the program is so new, figuring out which districts are applying—and therefore which placements are likely to pay student teachers—has been challenging. August 2026 reporting showed that the participation picture was still changing as applications came in. (Noonoo, 2026)

04
Goals of Schooling

What does an equitable education system owe its students—and its teachers?

Funding the teacher pipeline connects directly to educational equity, equal opportunity, access to qualified teachers, teacher diversity, and workforce development. If financial barriers determine who can afford to enter teaching—and shortages are concentrated in higher-need schools—then investing in teachers can also be understood as an investment in students. (Leung-Gagné et al., 2026) (Leung-Gagné et al., 2025)

Assignment Requirement: Relationship to the goals of schooling
01

Equal Opportunity

School finance affects whether students have access to comparable educational opportunities. In Serrano v. Priest, the California Supreme Court rejected a funding system in which educational opportunity depended heavily on district wealth. (Serrano v. Priest, 1971)

02

Access to Qualified Teachers

Students benefit when schools can recruit and retain fully prepared teachers. California's shortages and reliance on underprepared teachers are not distributed evenly, so teacher-pipeline funding is also an educational-equity issue. (Leung-Gagné et al., 2026)

03

Teacher Diversity & Workforce Development

Financial barriers can affect who is able to complete preparation and enter the profession. Reducing those barriers can strengthen the teacher pipeline, broaden access to the profession, and help schools build a stable workforce for students. (Leung-Gagné et al., 2025)

05
Teacher & Student Rights

What protections do teachers and students have around this topic?

Assignment Requirement: Rights + laws / legislation

Student teachers and legal protections

There are no specific protections requiring student teachers to be paid, so we can examine the broader teacher protections and education-funding laws that shape the profession and the public-school system in which student teachers prepare to work.

Serrano v. Priest
The California Supreme Court concluded that the state's then-existing school-finance system unconstitutionally made educational opportunity depend on district wealth. The case is foundational to California's effort to reduce wealth-based funding disparities. (Serrano v. Priest, 1971)
Proposition 13
Proposition 13 limits the basic property-tax rate to 1% and constrained local property-tax growth. Its fiscal effects helped move California toward greater state responsibility for school funding. (LAO, 2012)
Proposition 98
Proposition 98 creates a constitutional minimum annual funding requirement for K–12 schools and community colleges. It establishes the legal funding floor around which California builds its K–14 education budget. (LAO, 2026b)
LCFF / LCAP Requirements
LCFF provides a base grant and additional supplemental and concentration funding based on student need. The system reflects California's policy that higher-need student populations should receive additional resources, while local planning processes shape how those dollars are used. (CDE, 2023)
06
Who Makes the Decisions?

School finance is shaped at federal, state, and local levels.

Federal, state, and local actors can each make teacher-preparation funding a priority within the budgets and authority they control. In other words, support for student teachers can come from more than one level of government, depending on whether teacher preparation is treated as a funding priority and what each actor has the authority to do.

Assignment Requirement: Federal, state, and local actors
01

Federal

Congress and the U.S. Department of Education shape federal grants, financial-aid programs, and loan-relief policies. The federal government can help reduce the cost of becoming or remaining a teacher, but it does not run California's credential programs. (Federal Student Aid, n.d.-a)

02

California

The governor, California Legislature, California Department of Education, and Commission on Teacher Credentialing influence the state budget, school-funding rules, credential policy, and educator-workforce programs. These actors have the strongest direct role in creating statewide teacher-preparation investments. (CTC, 2026b)

03

Local

School boards, superintendents, school districts, and county offices of education make local spending and implementation decisions. When a state program requires local participation, those choices can determine whether funding actually reaches a candidate in a particular placement. (CTC, n.d.)

Universities / Credential Programs

Teacher-preparation programs, universities, and residency partnerships are also important implementation actors. They prepare candidates, coordinate clinical placements, and may help distribute or implement teacher-preparation funding even when the money itself comes from the federal or state government.

07
Where Does the Money Come From?

How do federal, state, and local funding work together?

In 2024–25, California K–12 public schools received about $142.4 billion from state, local, and federal sources. The state provided roughly 60%, local sources about 33%, and the federal government about 6% in a typical non-recession year. This broader school-finance structure provides the context for the more specific question of who currently helps pay to prepare teachers. (Lafortune & Guinan, 2025)

Assignment Requirement: Funding sources + relative quantities
01

State Funding — California

The state is the largest source of California K–12 revenue. California distributes most ongoing general-purpose school funding through the Local Control Funding Formula (LCFF), which provides a base grant per student and additional funding for higher-need populations. Supplemental grants add 20% for each unduplicated high-need student, and concentration funding increases support when high-need enrollment exceeds 55%. (CDE, 2026)

≈ 60% of K–12 funding
02

Local Funding

Local revenue—especially property taxes—contributes about one-third of California K–12 funding. Local property taxes interact with state funding because they count toward the Proposition 98 guarantee; when local property-tax revenue is higher, the state generally needs to contribute less from the General Fund. Local bonds and parcel taxes can provide additional locally approved revenue. (Lafortune & Guinan, 2025) (LAO, 2026b)

≈ 33% of K–12 funding
03

Federal Funding

Federal funding is a much smaller share of California K–12 revenue, but it is often targeted toward specific programs and student needs. Major federal education programs include Title I for schools serving high percentages of low-income families and IDEA funding connected to special education. Federal support also reaches individual teacher candidates through programs such as the TEACH Grant and Teacher Loan Forgiveness. (Lafortune & Guinan, 2025) (Federal Student Aid, n.d.-a)

≈ 6% of K–12 funding

Teacher-Preparation Funding

Who currently pays to prepare teachers?

Teacher candidates still pay many preparation costs themselves, including tuition, housing, transportation, credentialing expenses, and other living costs. At the same time, federal, state, and local programs now provide several forms of support. This mix of individual and public costs is at the center of our policy question: Should we pay to prepare the next generation of teachers?

F

Federal Support

TEACH Grant: The federal Teacher Education Assistance for College and Higher Education (TEACH) Grant is a federal student grant that provides up to $4,000 per year to eligible students who agree to teach for four years in a qualifying elementary school, secondary school, or educational service agency serving students from low-income families. If the service obligation is not met, the grant can be converted to a Direct Unsubsidized Loan. (Federal Student Aid, n.d.-a)

Teacher Loan Forgiveness: Teachers with eligible Direct Subsidized or Direct Unsubsidized Loans may apply for forgiveness after teaching full time for five complete and consecutive academic years in a qualifying low-income school or educational service agency. The program can provide up to $17,500 in federal loan forgiveness for qualifying teachers. (Federal Student Aid, n.d.-b)

CA

California Support

Student Teacher Stipend Program: California initially set aside $300 million in the July 2025 education budget to establish the Student Teacher Stipend Program beginning July 1, 2026. For the 2026–27 academic year, program funding was increased to $125 million. Eligible unpaid student teachers may receive a $10,000 stipend when their educator-preparation program and local education agency participate in the state process. (CTC, 2026b)

CTA Individual and Team Grants: The California Teachers Association's Institute for Teaching offers competitive grants for eligible CTA members to create student-centered projects. Grants are available for up to $5,000 for individuals and $20,000 for teams. (California Teachers Association [CTA], n.d.)

OC

Orange County / Local Support

OCDE Classified Employee Grant: The Orange County Department of Education, working with the Commission on Teacher Credentialing, offers eligible classified employees pursuing a teaching credential $3,500 per year in financial support. This is a local example of funding a pathway into teaching. (Martinez, 2025)

For the statewide Student Teacher Stipend Program, local districts and charter schools may administer the stipend, but the stipend itself is funded by California rather than created as a separate Orange County funding source. (CTC, n.d.)
